CareHalo

An AI-powered care enquiry and escalation system.

I wanted to find out whether AI could answer an important care enquiry when nobody was available — and turn that conversation into something a human could actually act on.

Watch demo ↗ Open prototype ↗

Automated call handling itself is not new. IVR systems and automated telephone menus have existed for years — from “press 1 for…” menus to systems that recognise particular words or intents and route a caller accordingly.

What interested me about the newer generation of AI voice agents was the ability to understand and retain context across a more natural conversation.

A caller might simply explain that they are calling about a parent who is currently in hospital and that the family is looking for residential care. Rather than forcing the caller through a predefined menu, an AI voice agent can understand who they are talking about, what the situation is, continue asking relevant questions and retain that context as the conversation develops.

That was the technology I wanted to understand by actually building a working model.

I used care enquiries as the scenario because they involve exactly this kind of free-flowing conversation. Someone may call about respite care, hospital discharge, dementia support, residential care or simply describe a family situation without knowing the exact terminology.

24%of enquiries in one 2023 analysis occurred outside standard office hours.
67%of new enquiries in a 2024 analysis were made by telephone.
Published care-sector research from 2023–2024. I use these figures as historical context, not as a current 2026 benchmark.

I also came across sophisticated, fully developed products already operating in this space, including Eliza by Lottie and Maxxo.ai. Seeing what was already possible made me more interested in understanding the technology underneath it. Rather than trying to build a production-ready platform, I wanted to create a working end-to-end model and learn how the different layers connect.

Introducing CareHalo

I gave the workflow a name: CareHalo.

I wanted the name to reflect the role I imagined the technology playing.

CareThe person making the enquiry — and the person who may need support — stays at the centre.
HaloThe technology sits around the enquiry as a support layer: listening, capturing and keeping the information moving until a human can act.
Care + Halo = CareHalo

Every call understood. Every need connected.

The person and the need stay at the centre; the technology supports the enquiry around them.

Phase 1Building the voice layer

The first part was the phone conversation.

I initially tested a voice-AI platform called Vapi, which helped me understand how an AI phone agent could be configured and connected. It was useful for getting started, but the voice experience I was getting was not quite right for the calm, natural British customer-care style I wanted for this model.

So I continued exploring other options and moved the working model to ElevenLabs, a platform for building conversational voice agents.

That setup helped me think about the agent as four separate layers working together:

LayerWhat it controls
VoiceWhat the caller actually hears.
System promptHow the agent should behave and what it should do.
Knowledge baseThe service-specific information available to the agent.
LLMThe reasoning model behind the conversation, selected within the ElevenLabs agent setup.

Voice

I searched using terms such as British English, customer care, customer support, conversational, calm and natural.

I tested different options in conversation rather than choosing purely from the voice description. For this use case, I wanted something clear and natural enough for somebody who might be discussing a care need rather than interacting with a typical sales or support bot.

System prompt

Inside ElevenLabs, the system prompt provides the instructions the AI agent follows during the conversation.

For the model, it establishes the agent's role, how it should communicate, what information it should try to collect, what it should do when something is unclear and how it should respond to potentially urgent situations.

Instruction areaExample in the model
RoleHandle an incoming care enquiry.
ToneCalm, clear and conversational.
Information captureCaller, person requiring care, relationship, care requirement, current situation and timing.
ClarificationAsk a follow-up question rather than guessing.
Contact detailsCarefully capture and confirm telephone or email information.
UrgencyRecognise situations that may need quicker human attention.

I refined these instructions while testing conversations, particularly around how the agent asked follow-up questions and confirmed information.

Knowledge base

The system prompt tells the agent how to behave. The knowledge base gives it information about the service.

For safe testing, I created a fictional care home called Meadowbrook and added test information covering areas such as care types, respite care, dementia support, general service information and common questions.

So if a caller asks whether respite care is available, the agent has service-specific information available to work from rather than relying only on the model's general knowledge.

LLM

The LLM is already part of the ElevenLabs agent configuration. I did not build a separate LLM application and connect it to ElevenLabs.

Within the ElevenLabs setup, the language model acts as the reasoning layer behind the agent — interpreting what the caller says, following the instructions and generating the next response.

VoiceWhat the caller hears.
System promptThe instructions.
Knowledge baseThe service-specific information.
LLMThe model reasoning through the conversation.

For the working model, Gemini 2.5 Flash was used within the ElevenLabs agent setup. The model selector also made another part of voice AI visible: different LLMs come with different latency and usage costs.

Phase 2Building the application

Once the voice conversation could collect the enquiry, I needed somewhere for that information to go.

I wanted the call to become something structured and useful rather than ending as just a transcript or recording.

For this part I used Bubble, a no-code application-development platform, to build the dashboard, database and workflow logic around the model.

I also experimented with Bubble's AI Agent to help accelerate parts of the initial application and UI build, then refined the structure around the information I wanted the model to capture and display.

Phase 3Building the follow-up

The final part was making sure the enquiry did not simply sit inside the application.

I wanted the workflow to notify the human team and handle an urgent enquiry differently from a standard one.

For this I connected SendGrid for email notifications and Twilio for the additional phone-escalation route when an enquiry is marked urgent.

EnquiryWhat happens
StandardBubble → SendGrid → email notification → human follow-up.
UrgentBubble → SendGrid + Twilio → phone escalation → human follow-up.
The AI handles the initial conversation and information capture. The human team still makes the decision and takes the action.

Data and UK GDPR considerations

The prototype uses fictional/test data. A live care enquiry could contain personal information and health-related information, so a production deployment would need appropriate controls around where data is processed, who can access it, how long it is retained and how the caller is informed about the AI interaction.

PlatformData consideration
ElevenLabsConversation, audio and transcript processing and retention settings.
BubbleStructured enquiry data, authentication, privacy rules and retention.
SendGridKeep notification content to the minimum required.
TwilioTelephony data and recording / retention configuration.
Collect what is needed. Store what is needed. Keep it only as long as needed.

A real UK deployment would also need the appropriate lawful basis, special-category data controls, processor arrangements and a DPIA where required.

Costing the working model

I also wanted to understand the running-cost layers of the complete stack rather than stopping at “the prototype works”.

For an illustrative scenario of 20 calls per week at around 4 minutes per call, that is roughly 87 calls / 347 voice minutes per month.

What the working model demonstrates

CareHalo gave me a way to understand the complete flow rather than looking at each technology separately.

The prototype connects a natural-language phone conversation with agent instructions, service-specific knowledge, LLM reasoning, structured information capture, a manager-facing dashboard and different follow-up routes depending on the urgency of the enquiry.

It also made the distinction between a working model and a production system much clearer. A real implementation would require much more testing, monitoring, failure handling, security, data governance, operational ownership and integration with the systems a team already uses.
