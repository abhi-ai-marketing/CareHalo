I created an AI-powered voice enquiry and escalation system.

I wanted to test whether AI could answer important business phone enquiries when no one was available — and turn those conversations into structured information a human could actually act on.

Watch demo ↗ Open prototype ↗

What changed wasn’t automated calling. It was the conversation.

IVR menus have existed for years. What interested me was whether the newer generation of conversational AI voice agents could understand context across a more natural phone conversation.

Instead of forcing somebody through a predefined menu, these systems can increasingly understand what a caller is saying in normal language, retain context as the conversation develops and ask relevant follow-up questions.

A caller does not necessarily need to know the terminology a business expects. They can explain the situation in their own words. The AI can listen, understand the context, clarify what is unclear and capture the information that matters.

That was the technology I wanted to understand by actually building something.

I started with healthcare.

More specifically, I focused on the care sector.

Care enquiries gave me a particularly useful real-world scenario to test. A family member might call because their parent is currently in hospital and may need residential care. Someone else may be looking for dementia support, short-term respite or simply trying to understand what kind of support is available.

Often, the caller may not know the exact terminology. They explain the situation, rather than selecting a service from a menu. That makes context and clarification particularly important.

The out-of-hours problem

From day-to-day work around care enquiries, one recurring operational concern kept coming up for me: what happens to important phone calls outside normal working hours, during evenings and at weekends?

During the working day, somebody may be available to answer. But enquiries do not stop at 5pm. A family may be dealing with a hospital discharge or may have just reached the point where they are ready to ask for help.

The question I wanted to explore was simple: how do you make sure that enquiry is not lost simply because it arrived at the wrong time?

24% of enquiries in a 2023 TrustedCare analysis occurred outside standard office hours.
67% of new care enquiries in a 2024 TrustedCare analysis were made by telephone.

Historical context from TrustedCare Professional Services: 2023 out-of-hours analysis and 2024 contact-method analysis. I use these as historical context rather than a current 2026 benchmark.

Could AI cover that initial gap until a human was available?

Not by replacing the human relationship. By answering the call, understanding why somebody is calling, capturing the important information and making sure the enquiry keeps moving.

The current generation of voice AI is moving quickly.

Voice automation itself is not new. What is newer is the accessibility and quality of LLM-powered, context-aware voice agents — combining more natural synthetic voices, reasoning models and APIs that can connect the conversation to other business systems.

As I researched the space, I also looked at more developed products already operating in and around care enquiry handling. That mattered because I was not starting from the assumption that nobody else had seen the problem.

Eliza by Lottie

Lottie describes Eliza as an AI enquiry handler for care providers, with call handling and live chat that can cover out-of-hours, overflow or 24/7.

Maxxo.ai

Maxxo describes AI-powered enquiry management for UK care homes, including voice, conversation capture, summaries and notifications to care-home teams.

References: Eliza by Lottie — AI enquiry handling and Maxxo.ai — AI enquiry management.

These are considerably more developed commercial products. My objective was different: build a working end-to-end model so I could understand what sits underneath the experience — the voice, instructions, knowledge, reasoning, application layer and follow-up automation.

I called it CareHalo.

A support layer around the enquiry until a human can act.

Halo represents the intelligent layer around that enquiry: listening, capturing, structuring and keeping the information moving until somebody can respond.

01

Answer

Pick up the incoming phone enquiry and begin a natural conversation.

Phase 01

Building the voice layer with ElevenLabs.

ElevenLabs handled the AI phone conversation — combining a more natural voice with the agent configuration behind it.

I initially experimented with Vapi while learning how conversational phone agents could be configured and connected. For this particular model, I wanted a calmer and more natural British customer-care style, so I continued exploring and moved the working model to ElevenLabs.

That helped me understand that the voice itself is only one part of the experience.

Voice

I searched and tested options using terms such as British English, customer care, customer support, conversational, calm and natural.

I tested voices in actual conversations rather than choosing one purely from its description. For this use case, I wanted something that felt appropriate for somebody potentially discussing a personal care situation.

This needed to feel less robotic than an old IVR menu.

System prompt

The system prompt tells the agent how it should behave. For CareHalo, that includes its role, tone, what information it should try to capture, when it should ask for clarification and how it should react when a situation may need faster human attention.

RoleHandle an incoming care enquiry.
ToneCalm, clear and conversational.
Information captureCaller, person requiring care, relationship, care requirement, current situation and timing.
ClarificationAsk rather than guess.
Contact detailsCarefully capture and confirm telephone or email information.
UrgencyRecognise situations that may require quicker human attention.

I refined these instructions while testing calls, particularly around how the agent asked follow-up questions, waited for the caller and confirmed important information.

Knowledge base

The system prompt tells the agent how to behave.
The knowledge base tells it about the service.

For safe testing I created a fictional care home called Meadowbrook. The test knowledge included areas such as residential care, respite care, dementia support, common questions and general service information.

So if somebody asks whether respite care is available, the agent has service-specific information available rather than relying only on general AI knowledge.

LLM

The language model is already part of the ElevenLabs agent configuration. I did not build a separate LLM application and connect it afterwards.

The LLM acts as the reasoning layer behind the conversation — interpreting what the caller says, following the system instructions, using the available knowledge and determining the next response.

For the working model I used Gemini 2.5 Flash within ElevenLabs.

Phase 02

Building the application with Bubble.

Once the voice conversation could collect the enquiry, I needed somewhere useful for that information to go.

I did not want the result to be simply an audio recording or a transcript. The phone conversation needed to become an actionable enquiry.

For this part I used Bubble to build the application, database, dashboard and orchestration around the voice conversation.

I also experimented with Bubble's AI Agent to accelerate parts of the initial application structure and UI before refining the information hierarchy around what I wanted somebody reviewing the enquiry to see.

Stores the enquiryThe important information captured during the call becomes a structured record.
Creates a conversation summaryThe useful parts of the conversation are condensed into something somebody can understand quickly.
Suggests the recommended actionThe application surfaces what the next follow-up should be.
Gives the team a dashboardIncoming enquiries can be reviewed in one place rather than disappearing into individual call recordings.

Data & governance

UK GDPR considerations.

The working model uses fictional/test data. A real care enquiry could contain personal information and potentially health-related or other special-category information.

A production deployment would therefore need appropriate controls around what is collected, where the information is processed, who can access it, how long it is retained and how callers are informed about the AI interaction.

ElevenLabsConversation, audio and transcript processing and retention settings.
BubbleAuthentication, privacy rules, structured enquiry information and retention.
SendGridOnly the information necessary for the notification should be included.
TwilioTelephony information and any recording or retention configuration.
Collect what is needed.
Store what is needed.
Keep it only as long as needed.

A real UK deployment would also need the appropriate lawful basis, special-category data controls, processor arrangements and a DPIA where appropriate.

What I learned from building it.

The useful part of CareHalo was not getting one individual tool to work. It was understanding how all of the different layers need to connect.

A natural voice needs clear instructions.The sound of the agent matters, but behaviour comes from the prompt and the information available to it.
A good conversation still needs somewhere to go.The application layer is what turns speech into something operationally useful.
A dashboard alone is not enough.The system still needs a route for notifying a human and treating urgent enquiries differently.
The handover matters most.Automation is useful only when it is clear where AI stops and human judgement begins.

CareHalo is a working model, not a finished production platform. A real implementation would require considerably more testing, monitoring, failure handling, security, governance, integration and operational ownership.

Building the complete system helped me understand what has to happen between a phone ringing and a human receiving something genuinely useful from that conversation.
